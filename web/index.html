Covered is not the same as connected.

Coverage maps show where a phone should get signal on a normal day. They do not show what that signal depends on. Underlink reads the public radio licence register as a network and finds, for each remote Northern Territory community, the relays its service cannot route around.

In plain words: many remote communities get phone service through a line of radio towers on hills. If one tower in that line breaks and there is no way around it, the whole community can lose service, even when the map says it is covered.

Words we use

Relay
A radio tower that passes the phone signal on to the next tower.
Weak link (single-path relay)
A relay with no way around it. If it stops, everyone after it loses service.
Fibre town
A town on the fibre cable, where the signal joins the national network.
Radio chain
The line of relays that carries a community's signal to a fibre town.

Try it: break a link

This small network is made up. It works the same way as the analysis of the real one: 358 Telstra radio sites and 313 links from the ACMA register. Switch relays off and see which communities lose their path to a fibre town. Real relay locations are not shown anywhere on this site.

What each community depends on

    A single-path relay is a relay that every licensed radio path from a community to fibre must pass through. The code finds them with a dominator tree (NetworkX), which gives the same answer as switching each relay off in turn.

    What we found

    In plain words: 23 larger communities reach the phone network through a radio chain. 18 of them have at least one weak link, and about 7,100 people live there. For 16 of those 18 the coverage map says "covered".

    How long a repair could take

    In plain words: in the dry season most of these communities would be reconnected within a day. In the wet season, 17 of 23 could wait more than two weeks, because a tower on their chain is far from a sealed road.

    Days to restore = 0.5 days on site + travel from the nearest of five crew bases + a wet-season delay when a radio site on the chain is more than a set distance from a sealed road. The delay and the distance are our assumptions, so you can change them.

    For government: measures with a starting point

    In plain words: ask phone companies how each tower connects, how long its batteries last and when it has gone down, then check the weak links before every wet season.

    Each recommendation in the report comes with a measure, an owner and a target. Public data gives the starting value today.

    Three fields to ask carriers for

    When the NT Government co-invests in a site, it asks the carrier for three restricted fields per site:

    1. Backhaul type: fibre, radio or satellite
    2. Battery hours at the site and at each relay feeding it
    3. A three-year outage log

    This extends the restricted government access to asset locations that the 2024 Regional Telecommunications Review proposed (Recommendation 6).

    Method and data

    1. Build the network. 808 NT point-to-point link records licensed to Telstra become 358 sites and 313 links in 51 separate pieces.
    2. Trace each place to fibre. Every fibre-connected site joins one imaginary source; a dominator tree finds the relays each place's site cannot route around.
    3. Add four measures beside it. Cyclone replays, published backup power, a repair window from road access, and what still works nearby. They are shown side by side and never merged into one score.
    4. Check it. Nine sensitivity runs, the ACCC site list, and every outage reported in the news that we could find.
    5. Release by tier. Regional counts in public, a relay register for government and carriers only, and a card each community controls.

    Against reported outages

    The report tests the model against six outages that news reports described (Table 2). Two happened at places the model puts on chains of five single-path relays. It misses four, and each miss traces to data carriers hold and do not publish: battery hours, fibre routes and which sites serve which places.

    This site names no community. The report names them only because news reports did, and only to test the model.

      Limits

      • The ACMA register shows only licensed radio. Fibre and satellite backhaul are missing; links it cannot show can only lower the count for the 23 chains.
      • Fibre towns are assumed to be sound. The nearest radio site stands in for the serving site; the ACCC check above tests that.
      • Replays show exposure without probabilities. Repair parameters are assumptions and are swept.
      • Population figures are 2020 estimates, and homeland populations change with the seasons.

      Data rules

      Underlink studies a carrier's network, so every finding describes equipment, links and missing data. We follow the CARE principles and the Maiam nayri Wingara principles of Indigenous data sovereignty, and we avoid data that is blaming, aggregate, decontextualised, deficit-based and restricted (Walter's BADDR critique).

      Public (this site)

      Aggregate counts, two land council groups, the made-up network above. No relay locations, no site identifiers, no community rankings.

      Government and carriers

      The relay register with hashed identifiers, for DCDD and carrier field teams only.

      Each community

      Its own card and any outage records it chooses to keep. A custodian the community chooses approves or withholds anything about it. Automated steps cannot publish.

      • A test fails the build if any public table holds place keys or names.
      • Land tenure is used only to flag permit lead time (a section 19 lease on Aboriginal Land Trust land). No sacred-site data is used or inferred.
      • Community-language text is written and voiced by paid interpreters. No machine translation.
